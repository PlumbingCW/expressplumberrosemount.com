If water is spreading from your water heater in Rosemount, treat it as an emergency, and the line is answered 24/7. The first thing to do is stop the water feeding the tank and keep clear of the floor around it. Then call, and we will tell you what to do next while we set up a visit.

Most replacement calls are not a flood. They are a cold shower, a puddle that keeps coming back, or a tank that has started to knock. This page explains how to tell a tank that can be saved from one that cannot, what a same-day swap involves, and what moves the price.

Water on the floor right now? Do these first

A leaking tank can drop a lot of water in a short time, and the water inside is hot. Work through these in order and stop as soon as the water stops.

  • 1
    Smell for gas before anything else.If the heater is gas-fired and you smell gas, do not touch switches or appliances. Leave the building and call from outside. Minnesota Energy Resources customers can use the leak line at 800-889-4970. Xcel Energy customers should call 911 once outside.
  • 2
    Shut the cold supply to the tank.Find the valve on the cold pipe running into the top of the heater and turn it clockwise until it stops. If it will not move or you cannot find it, close the main shutoff where the water line enters the house.
  • 3
    Cut power to an electric heater.Switch off the dedicated breaker for the heater. Never leave an electric element powered if the tank is draining, because a dry element burns out fast.
  • 4
    Keep people and pets away from hot water.Water coming from the relief valve pipe or the tank seam can scald. Stay out of the puddle until the supply is closed and the tank has had time to cool.
  • 5
    Move what you can and call us.Lift boxes, rugs and stored items off the floor. Then call the 24/7 line so we can plan a swap, often the same day when the schedule allows.

If you cannot stop the flow, the main shutoff or the city can help. The city says Rosemount Public Works can shut off service if a plumber advises it is necessary.

Replace it or repair it: how the decision usually falls

A heater that leaks from the tank itself is done. The steel shell has rusted through, and no part can fix that. Repair makes sense when a single part has failed on a tank that is otherwise sound, such as a thermostat, an element or a valve.

Age matters. The data plate on the side lists the maker, the model and a serial number that shows when the tank was built. If it is old and the repair is not small, putting money into it usually buys a few months, not a few seasons.

A good test is to ask what else is likely to go next. A tank with a bad element, a rusty drain valve and sediment piled in the bottom has several problems waiting. A younger tank with one failed part is worth fixing, and we will say so on the phone.

If the tank is sound and only the heat is missing, read our page on water heater repair before you commit to a new unit.

Four signs the tank is on its way out

These show up before a tank fails outright. Catching them early lets you plan a swap on a weekday instead of at two in the morning.

Rust-colored hot water.

If only the hot side runs brown or orange, the inside of the tank is corroding. Cold water that runs clear while hot does not points straight at the heater, not the pipes.

Moisture at the base.

A damp or rusty ring around the bottom, or a wet drain pan, can mean a leak from the tank. Check that the leak is not just a dripping fitting on top that runs down the side.

Rumbling or popping.

Sediment settles on the floor of the tank and traps water under it. That water boils, and the sound is a rumble or pop each time the burner or element runs.

Hot water that runs out early.

A tank packed with sediment holds less hot water than its label says. Short showers and a heater that never quite catches up are the usual signs.

One sign alone is a reason to look closer. Two or three together are a reason to start pricing a replacement.

What a same-day swap involves, in order

The visit is a routine job for a plumber, and most of the time goes into doing the finishing steps correctly rather than moving the tank.

1

Shut off and drain.

The plumber closes the water supply, turns off power or fuel, and drains the old tank to a floor drain or through a hose outside.

2

Disconnect and haul away.

Water lines, the gas line or electrical connection, and the relief valve pipe come off. The old tank leaves with the plumber, so you are not left with a dead shell in the basement.

3

Set the new tank.

The new heater goes on a level spot, ideally with a drain pan beneath it. New connectors, a new relief valve and any code-required fittings go on.

4

Check the vent or flue.

On a gas unit, the vent path must draw properly and slope correctly, and the connections must be tight. This is a safety check, not a formality.

5

Fill, relight and test.

The tank fills with the hot tap open to push air out. The plumber then restores power or gas, lights or starts the unit, and checks every joint for leaks and the temperature at the tap.

If your plan involves moving the heater, changing fuel or adding venting, that is a planned project and fits better under water heater installation.

What 17-grain water does to a Rosemount tank

The City of Rosemount supplies water from its own groundwater wells, and the city lists the hardness at 17 grains per gallon. That is considered hard water. Hard water leaves mineral scale on heating surfaces and a layer of sediment on the bottom of the tank.

For your decision, this means a tank in Rosemount works harder and may wear sooner than the same tank on softer water. If an old tank is rumbling, scale is the likely cause, and it also tells you the replacement will face the same conditions.

A new tank gives you a chance to build a habit. Flushing a few quarts from the drain valve once or twice a year carries some sediment out. Many tanks also have a sacrificial anode rod that corrodes first to protect the steel, and checking or replacing it is the best defense against early failure.

A softener changes the load on the heater, which is why some owners pair one with a new tank. The city's own water report lists pros and cons, so read it before you decide. Our page on water softeners and filtration covers the choice.

Source: City of Rosemount FAQ and Water page (hardness, undated), 2025 Rosemount Drinking Water Report (softening pros and cons).

Gas or electric: choosing the fuel for the new tank

Most replacements stay with the fuel already in the house, because switching adds work. Here is how the two common choices differ.

Gas-fired tank.

  • Needs a gas line, a shutoff and a vent or flue that draws correctly.
  • Recovers hot water quickly after heavy use.
  • Requires a working vent path, so the swap includes a safety check.
  • Gas service comes from Minnesota Energy Resources or Xcel Energy, depending on your address.

Electric tank.

  • Needs a dedicated circuit and a breaker of the right size.
  • No flue, so it can sit in more locations.
  • Recovers more slowly, which matters for larger households.
  • A failed element or thermostat is often a repair, not a replacement.

Tankless units are a third path. They have their own sizing and gas questions, covered under tankless water heaters.

What moves the price of a replacement

We give you a clear price before work starts, and the number depends on a handful of things you can see for yourself.

Fuel and unit type.A gas unit with venting is a different job from an electric tank, and a power-vented or sealed-combustion model changes the scope again.
Tank capacity.A larger tank costs more to buy and can need more room, longer connectors or a bigger vent.
Condition of the connections.Corroded shutoffs, old connectors and a failing gas line fitting get replaced during the swap, and each one adds material and time.
Venting and code items.A vent that needs rework, a missing drain pan, a relief valve that discharges nowhere safe or an expansion tank to add all change the scope.
Access and haul-away.A tight crawlspace, a steep basement stair or a unit in an attic changes the time. Hauling the old tank is part of the job.
Timing.A planned swap on a weekday is easier to schedule than a night call. The price is still given clearly before we begin.

If a sudden leak damaged floors or walls, ask your insurer how it treats the loss. Take photos of the tank and the water before you clean up, and keep the old unit's data plate details.
