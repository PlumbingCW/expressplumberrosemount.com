Apple Valley Water Is Harder Than Rosemount's

The city of Apple Valley reports about 24 grains per gallon of hardness in its drinking water, in a table updated March 2024. Rosemount's city pages give 17 grains. That gap is large, and it means scale builds faster on Apple Valley heating elements, tank bottoms and faucet aerators.

The city draws from wells, most about 500 feet into the Jordan aquifer and some over 1,000 feet into the Mt. Simon-Hinckley aquifer. Its treatment plant removes iron and manganese but does not soften the water. If you have not added a softener, every fixture in the house sees that full hardness.

The practical result is that a water heater here may need service sooner than the same model would in a softer town. A tank that rumbles, a heater that takes longer to recover, or a shower head that clogs every few months all point the same direction. When the heater quits at night, water heater repair is one of the first calls we get from this side of the county.

Plumbing work in Apple Valley follows the same state code as Rosemount, which is the Minnesota Plumbing Code (Rules chapter 4714). The city's Building Inspections office under Public Works is where to ask about permits.

Before We Arrive at Your Apple Valley Home

A few minutes of prep makes the visit faster and keeps damage down. These cards fit the older single-family homes common across Apple Valley.

1

Find the main shutoff.

In many 1960s and 1970s houses the main valve sits near the water meter in the basement or utility room. Turn it clockwise until it stops. If it will not move, leave it alone and tell us when we answer.

2

Clear the way to the heater.

Move boxes and storage away from the water heater and the floor drain. Older homes often have the heater in a tight utility corner, and open space saves time.

3

Note what the drain is doing.

Tell us whether one fixture is slow or every drain gurgles. A single slow sink often points to a branch line. Several fixtures at once points toward the main line out to the street.

4

Stop adding water.

Do not run the dishwasher, washer or extra showers while a drain is backing up. Every gallon you add has to go somewhere, and in a backup that somewhere is your floor.

5

Protect what you can lift.

Pull rugs, cardboard and anything on the floor away from the wet area. If the water heater tank itself is leaking, turn off its power or gas control. If you smell gas, leave the building and call 911 from outside.

6

Have the house age ready.

If you know roughly when the house was built, say so. It helps us picture the pipe material and the likely layout before we arrive.

Why Apple Valley's Older Homes Call for a Careful Look

The city's own plan describes Apple Valley as a mature suburb, with many neighborhoods of single-family homes built in the 1960s and 1970s. It also says about 52 percent of housing units, counted through 2016, were built before 1987, and that these are mostly single-family. Residential building here began in the mid-1950s and picked up in the 1960s along County Road 42.

That age matters for a plumber's first look. Houses from those decades may still have original cast iron or clay drain lines, an older water heater, or galvanized or early copper supply pipe in places. We say may, because every house is different and many have been updated. The only way to know is to look, and a camera inspection of the drain is often the quickest way to see the pipe.

If an older drain is slow again and again, a repair may be the better plan than another cleaning. Our drain cleaning page explains when clearing the line is enough and when a camera and a closer look make sense. Roots, grease and wipes are the causes the city itself lists for sewer backups.

Who Owns What: The City Line and Your Line

Apple Valley Public Works runs the sewer collection system and cleans about one-third of the city's lines each year. Wastewater then goes on to disposal facilities owned by the Metropolitan Council. The homeowner is responsible for the service line from the house to the city main, including roots and debris.

On city streets, the owner's repair responsibility runs up to and including the connection at the property line. The city says it is liable only if its own negligence caused a backup. For a backup, that boundary decides who pays, so it is worth a quick check before you hire anyone.

  • Call Apple Valley Public Works first if the whole street may be affected. The city asks residents to do this before hiring a drain cleaner.
  • If only your house is backing up, the cause is usually on your side of the line.
  • Ask us to describe what a camera shows so you can show the city if the problem is at the main.
