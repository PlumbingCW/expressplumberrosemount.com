Sewage Coming Up? Do These Things First

A backup gets worse with every gallon sent down the line. These steps protect your home and your family while you wait.

  • 1
    Stop every source of water.No flushing, showers, dishwasher or sinks. That includes the washing machine, which sends a large volume at once.
  • 2
    Keep people out of the affected area.Sewage carries bacteria. Keep children and pets away from the basement, and do not touch the water with bare hands.
  • 3
    Do not run the washer or dryer cycle.A washer drains straight into the same blocked line. Stop a load already in progress if you can do it safely.
  • 4
    Stay out of standing water near electrical items.If water is near outlets, a furnace or the panel, do not walk through it. Shut off power at the panel only if it is dry and safe to reach.
  • 5
    Open a window and keep the area ventilated.A sewer smell is unpleasant, and good airflow helps while you wait.

Same-day appointments are offered when available, and the line is answered nights and weekends, so a backup late on a Saturday is not left waiting for Monday.

Why the Floor Drain and Lowest Fixtures Back Up First

Sewage follows gravity. When the main line is blocked, wastewater has nowhere to go and rises to the lowest opening in the house. In most homes that is the basement floor drain, a basement shower, or the lowest toilet.

Gurgling is the earlier warning. Air trapped in a blocked line pushes out through the nearest trap, so you may hear the toilet bubble when the tub drains. Several fixtures slowing at once, or one drain affecting another, points past a simple fixture clog.

If only one sink is slow, the problem is probably local and a drain cleaning is enough. See drain cleaning for how that differs from a main line job.

Signs the Main Line Is Blocked

Some of these signs show up days before a full backup. Catching them early can keep the mess out of your basement.

Basement floor drain overflows.

Water or sewage rising in the floor drain is the clearest sign of a blocked main.

Multiple fixtures drain slowly.

Toilets, tubs and sinks all slow down together.

Gurgling or bubbling.

Air pushed back through traps makes noise at the toilet or tub.

Toilet water level changes.

The bowl rises, falls or bubbles when another fixture drains.

Sewer smell indoors.

A persistent smell near the floor drain or a basement bath.

Wet or lush spot in the yard.

A soggy patch or unusually green grass over the line can point to a leak or a blockage.

Your Pipe, the City's Main: Where Responsibility Changes

The city of Rosemount runs the public sewer, and homeowners own the private pipe from the house to the street sewer main. The city's Sanitary Sewer page says property owners are responsible for backups in that service line.

The city also camera-inspects its own sewer mains every three years. That is a practical clue when a backup happens. If a plumber clears your line at the street and the problem is gone, the blockage was in the private pipe. If the city main were the problem, neighbors on the same street would usually be affected too.

If you suspect the main under the street is the problem, such as several homes backing up at once, call the city utilities office as well as us. We can clear and inspect your private line either way, and the camera recording helps show where the blockage sits.

Source: City of Rosemount Sanitary Sewer page (rosemountmn.gov)

Roots in the Line: When Cleaning Is Only a Stopgap

Tree roots find small gaps at pipe joints and grow inside the pipe looking for water. A cable with a cutting head clears the roots out, and the line works again. The roots grow back, often within a year or two, and the cycle repeats.

That makes cleaning a stopgap when roots entered through a damaged joint. A camera shows where roots enter and how the pipe looks around them. If the joint is cracked or offset, repair is the lasting answer, and the plumber will explain the options on the sewer line repair page.

If the pipe is sound and roots are light, regular cleaning may be enough. Some owners clean on a schedule rather than dig up a yard, and that is a fair choice when the pipe passes the camera look.

How a Sewer Line Cleaning Visit Goes

A typical sewer line cleaning follows three stages.

1

Find the blockage.

The plumber checks the lowest drain and the cleanout to learn how far the blockage is from the house.

2

Clear the line.

A cable or jetter opens the line. Roots are cut and debris is pulled out or flushed to the main.

3

Look and explain.

A camera confirms the line is clear and shows roots, cracks or low spots. You hear whether cleaning is enough.
