Water under the sink: shut it off first

Stopping the water takes a minute and saves the cabinet, the floor and often the ceiling below. Work through these in order.

  • 1
    Close the fixture valve.Turn the oval handle on the supply line clockwise by hand. Do not put a wrench on an old valve, because forcing it can snap the stem and make the leak worse.
  • 2
    Use the main valve if the fixture valve fails.Find the main shutoff where the water line enters the house, often in a basement or utility area. Close it fully, then open a low faucet to let the pipes drain.
  • 3
    Protect the space.Empty the cabinet, lay down towels and set a bucket under any drip. Keep your hands and tools away from wet outlets.
  • 4
    Cut power near standing water.If water reached an outlet or a disposal, leave that circuit off at the panel until a plumber has looked at it.
  • 5
    Call the 24/7 line.Tell us what you shut off and whether the water has stopped. We will tell you whether this can wait for a same-day appointment when available or needs a visit right away.

A burst supply line is the true emergency in this trade. Braided lines can fail at the crimped end with no warning and release full house pressure into a cabinet. Everything else on this page can usually wait until a scheduled visit.

Four faucet complaints and what each one means

The symptom narrows the cause before anyone opens the faucet. Here is how we read each one.

Drip from the spout.

Water is slipping past a worn seal, seat or cartridge when the handle is off. A drip wastes water all day and wears the part further, so it is worth fixing soon.

Spray or seep at the handle or base.

This points to worn O-rings or a cracked body. Water at the base of the faucet can also mean a loose mounting nut, so we check that before replacing parts.

Low flow or a split stream.

A clogged aerator is the usual cause on a single faucet. If hot and cold are both weak at every tap in the house, the problem is upstream of the sink.

Shutoff valve that will not close.

Old valves seize when they sit unused. Forcing one can break it, so we replace the valve once the main is closed and the line is drained.

Why Rosemount water leaves scale in aerators and cartridges

The city lists Rosemount water at 17 grains per gallon and calls it hard. As that water evaporates at the spout, minerals stay behind. They crust over the aerator screen and collect inside cartridges and between ceramic discs, which is why a weak stream or a stiff handle shows up in so many local kitchens.

The city also flushes all of its hydrants each spring. It says a temporary drop in water pressure and some discoloration is normal afterward. If every faucet in the house runs weak or cloudy at that time, run the cold taps for a few minutes and wait before you replace any part.

Swapping a faucet is a light job, yet changes to supply lines and shutoffs still fall under the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC) as adopted in Minnesota Rules chapter 4714. A licensed plumber knows where a quick swap ends and a code question begins.

Clear the aerator before you pay for a visit

This takes ten minutes and fixes a large share of low-flow complaints. Clear the aerator first, because it costs nothing and rules out the simplest cause.

1

Unscrew the aerator.

Turn the small cap at the tip of the spout counterclockwise. Wrap a cloth around it if you need pliers, so the finish does not scratch.

2

Soak and brush it.

Let the screen sit in white vinegar for an hour, then scrub off the loosened scale with an old toothbrush.

3

Flush the spout and refit.

Run the water with the aerator off to clear loose grit, then screw it back on snug by hand.

If the stream is still weak with a clean aerator, the cause is inside the faucet or in the supply line. That is the point where a plumber's visit pays off. If scale keeps returning, our page on water softeners and filtration explains the longer-term options.

Supply lines and shutoff valves live under the sink

The flexible supply lines and the oval valves under a sink do the quiet work that a faucet depends on. Replace a line when it kinks, bulges, or shows rust stains at the fittings. A valve that has not moved in years may weep at the stem or refuse to close, and that is the moment to swap it.

A leak that returns after a dry-looking cabinet check may be coming from somewhere you cannot see. Our leak detection service tracks down hidden wet spots. If a line has actually burst, see burst pipe repair for how we handle it.

Adding a new sink or moving one calls for new supply and drain connections. That work belongs under plumbing installation, not a faucet repair.

Repair or replace: how we decide

A repair is not always the smart spend. We tell you which side your faucet is on before work starts.

Repair usually wins when.

  • The faucet body is sound and only a cartridge, seat or washer is worn.
  • Parts are easy to get for the make.
  • The finish is in good shape.
  • The problem is a single drip or a stiff handle.

Replacement usually wins when.

  • The body is cracked or corroded at the base.
  • Parts are discontinued or cost close to a new faucet.
  • The finish is flaking or pitted.
  • You are already redoing the counter or sink.

The same sink often hides other trouble. If the disposal beside the faucet is humming or leaking, see garbage disposal repair. For a toilet in the same bathroom, our toilet repair page covers the usual faults.
