Spot Repair, Lining, Pipe Bursting or Full Replacement

Sewer repair is not one job. These are the four main paths, and the right one depends on how much of the pipe is damaged and where.

Spot repair.

A short damaged section is cut out and replaced. It fits a single break or offset joint, usually where the camera shows a clear problem spot.

Trenchless lining.

A resin-coated liner is pulled through the old pipe and cured in place, forming a new pipe inside the old one. It suits pipes that are cracked or leaking but still in line.

Pipe bursting.

A new pipe is pulled through the old path while the old pipe is broken apart. It suits a pipe too damaged for lining but with a straight run.

Full replacement.

The old pipe is dug out and a new one laid. It fits collapsed, badly offset or heavily bellied lines that other methods cannot correct.

Trenchless methods are not always possible. A line that has collapsed, has sharp bends, or has a section that must change slope may need a trench.

How the Camera Look Decides the Repair

The recording from the camera is the basis for everything that follows. The plumber walks the line and then explains the choice.

1

Clear the line.

If the pipe is blocked, it is cleaned first so the camera can pass. See sewer inspection for what the camera can and cannot show.

2

Record the pipe.

The camera shows pipe material, cracks, offsets, roots, and low spots, and marks where each one sits along the line.

3

Match the repair.

Short damage points to spot repair. A long run of cracks may point to lining or bursting. A collapse or major offset points to replacement.

Cast Iron Sections Inside the House

Many older Minnesota homes have cast iron drain pipe inside the walls and under the basement floor. Cast iron lasts a long time, but it can rust through from the inside, crack, or lose its joints. Signs include rusty stains at joints, slow drains, and a sewer smell near the floor.

The camera can reach the inside of cast iron too, showing flaking, scale and cracks. A bad section can be cut out and replaced with plastic pipe using proper couplings. That is often a spot repair rather than a whole-house job.

Because cast iron work is inside the building, it affects floors and walls. The plumber will explain which areas need to be opened and what the repair involves before work starts.

Sewer repairs on your private line are governed by the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC) chapters that Minnesota Rules chapter 4714 makes part of state law. The state requires altered plumbing systems to be inspected and tested, and the digging itself needs a Gopher State One Call locate under Minnesota Statutes chapter 216D.

Who Owns the Pipe and a Common Mix-Up

The city of Rosemount says homeowners own the private pipe from the house to the street sewer main. That means a repair on your lateral is your decision and your cost, and the city is responsible for the main under the street.

Do not confuse this with the city's lead service line inventory. That project is about water service lines, the small pipes that bring drinking water into the house. It does not cover sewer laterals, so a notice about it has no bearing on a sewer repair.

If you are unsure where your private pipe ends and the main begins, ask the city. A camera look shows the line up to the street connection, which helps place any damage on your side.

Source: City of Rosemount Sanitary Sewer page and Service Line Inventory page (rosemountmn.gov)

Call 811 Before Any Digging

Minnesota law requires you to call Gopher State One Call at 811 before you dig. The state asks for at least 48 hours' notice, not counting weekends and holidays, and the locate service is free to the excavator.

A sewer repair that involves a trench or an access pit needs this call. Gas, electric, water and communication lines may run near the sewer lateral, and hitting one is dangerous and expensive.

A contractor normally handles the call for the job, but it is worth asking. If you plan any digging yourself, call first and wait for the marks.
