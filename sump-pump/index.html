Most sump failures come down to a few causes: a stuck float, a clogged intake, a failed check valve, a dead motor or a lost power supply. Some of these take minutes to fix. Others mean a new pump, and we will say which one it is before any work starts.

Basin Rising Right Now? Do These Things First

Water that has reached the top third of the basin needs attention today. These steps protect you while the plumber is on the way.

  • 1
    Stay out of standing water.If water covers the floor near outlets, a power strip or the panel, do not walk through it. Electricity and wet concrete are a bad mix.
  • 2
    Check the power the safe way.If the cord is dry and you can reach the outlet without wading, look for a tripped GFCI button or breaker. Reset it once. If it trips again, leave it off and tell us.
  • 3
    Lift valuables and clear the floor.Move boxes, stored paper and electronics up onto shelves or tables. Pull up area rugs so water does not wick into them.
  • 4
    Look at the float and the discharge pipe.With the lid off and the pump unplugged, check whether the float arm is stuck against the wall. Do not reach into the basin while the pump is plugged in.
  • 5
    Call and describe what you see.Tell the plumber whether the pump hums, runs without moving water, or makes no sound at all. That helps us arrive with the right parts.

If water is entering fast from a burst pipe rather than the ground, shut off the main water valve and read our page on burst pipe repair while you wait.

Signs Your Sump Pump Is Failing

A pump rarely dies without warning. Most give one or more of these signals, and a thaw or a heavy storm is when the weak ones quit.

Runs constantly.

A pump that never shuts off may have a stuck float, a failed check valve or a basin too small for the water coming in. Constant running also wears out the motor.

Cycles on and off in seconds.

Short cycling usually means water is flowing back down the discharge pipe because the check valve is bad. The pump pushes the same water out again and again.

Loud grinding or rattling.

Noise often means debris in the impeller or worn bearings. A rattle can also come from a loose discharge pipe.

Basin water keeps rising.

If the pump runs and the level still climbs, the intake may be clogged or the pump is too weak for the load.

Hums but moves no water.

A humming pump with no discharge can have a seized impeller or a frozen or blocked outside pipe.

Nothing happens at all.

No sound and no movement points to power loss, a dead motor or a float that is not switching on.

Where the Water Goes After the Pump Matters in Rosemount

Discharge rules vary from city to city, and we do not quote a Rosemount rule here because the city's own pages do not spell one out. A common restriction in many places is that a sump pump may not connect to the sanitary sewer. Before you connect or reroute a discharge line, confirm the requirement with the city Building Permits and Inspections office at 651-322-2024.

The city's 2007 sanitary sewer plan lists sump pumps and roof drains as inflow into the sanitary system. That is a planning statement and not an ordinance, but it shows why the city cares where this water ends up. Clean groundwater in the sewer takes up capacity that the city and the Metropolitan Council pay to move and treat.

Twin Puddles Park on Dodd Boulevard is named for the lack of storm sewer in that neighborhood, according to the city. If your home sits in an area like that, a discharge line that dumps near the foundation can send water straight back to the basin. We plan the outlet so water travels away from the house and does not freeze in the pipe.

Source: City of Rosemount Building Permits and Inspections page, the 2007 city sanitary sewer system plan, and city park facility page for Twin Puddles Park.

Repair the Pump or Replace It

A pump is a wear item. Here is how we think about the choice.

Repair makes sense when.

  • The problem is a stuck float, a clogged intake or a bad check valve.
  • The pump is quiet and moves water well once cleared.
  • The motor is not overheating or tripping the breaker.

Replace makes sense when.

  • The motor hums but will not turn, or trips the breaker.
  • The pump runs constantly and the basin still rises.
  • It is old enough that another failure in the next storm is likely.

Replacing in the same visit usually costs less than a repair followed by a second emergency call. We tell you which path fits before we start.

How a Sump Pump Visit Goes

The visit follows the same order every time so nothing is missed.

1

Find out why it stopped.

We check power, the float, the intake and the pump body. We also look at the basin size and the pipe leaving it.

2

Clear and test.

We clear debris, pour water into the basin and watch the full cycle. A pump that starts and stops cleanly passes.

3

Check the check valve.

We confirm the valve holds water in the pipe and does not let it fall back. A failed valve is a common reason a good pump still struggles.

4

Review the discharge line.

We trace the pipe outside, look for kinks or freeze risk, and check where the water lands.

5

Explain the options.

You hear the repair choices and the price before work starts. Nothing is replaced without your approval.

Battery Backup and Check Valves: The Two Upgrades That Pay Off

Storms often knock out power at the same moment the pump is working hardest. A battery backup pump sits in the basin beside the main pump and takes over if power fails or the main pump quits. It does not run forever, so it buys time and does not replace the grid.

A good check valve keeps discharged water from flowing back into the basin. It is cheap compared with a pump and it saves wear on the motor. We install them with a union fitting so they can be replaced without cutting pipe later.
